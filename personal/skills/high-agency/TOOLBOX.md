# Toolbox

Escape tools for when a step stalls or a problem won't move. Reach for the one that matches the stuck pattern — don't run the whole list. Each tool is a question or a move that recruits agency the user already has.

## Defy the laws of physics?

When the user (or the world) says it's impossible. Ask: *Does solving this defy the laws of physics?* If no, it is solvable — the only question is human effort, not fixed odds. Shannon and Thorp decided beating roulette didn't break physics, so they built a wearable computer and beat the house. Use this to wipe the mental whiteboard before planning.

## Inversion (the guy on the left)

When the user is overcomplicating — researching the perfect keyboard instead of writing. Don't try to be the clever guy. Ask: *How would I guarantee the worst possible outcome?* List the failure moves, then flip them. "Become a worse writer → don't write, write inconsistently, write about boring things" flips to the three simple actions that actually matter. Strips midwit complexity down to what the guy on the left would just do.

## The 10x-agency twin

When the user is welded to a past assumption and can only see one move. Ask: *What would I do if I had 10x the agency?* Imagine an identical twin whose only job is 10x your agency — what would they try? Throws a first-principles grenade at the assumptions. The bouncer said no: the twin bribes them, finds the back door, calls the owner, stages a fake TMZ crew. Surfaces options the assumption was hiding.

## What is level 1?

When the user is overwhelmed by the gap between now and the goal. Turn it into a video game. Ask: *What's the smallest first step — level 1?* Level 1 must be small enough to feel trivial; level 2 is often "use level 1 to design the next levels." Momentum from a tiny win beats a perfect master plan. (This is the engine behind the Plan step — use it whenever a level still feels too big.)

## Decisions as experiments

When the user is ruminating, hunting a perfect risk-free option. Reframe: they are not a perfectionist on stage, they are a scientist testing a hypothesis. "I'm 60% sure New York beats staying — book the ticket and run the experiment." Success is *running the test*, not being right. Rumination is a smoke alarm: when it fires, answer with *how can I take action on this now?*

## Burn the boats

When intention is real but action never starts (the New Year's-resolution failure mode). Manufacture urgency and consequence:
- **Public deadline** — announce it; invite people to the calendar event.
- **Ticking clock** — tie the reward to time, so delay visibly costs (Musk's $1M-a-month-decay offer to Hotz).
- **Bet on yourself** — put real stakes behind not-doing.
Well-meaning intent doesn't create action; a gun behind your head does.

## The story razor

When stuck between two roughly-equal options. Ask: *Which makes the better story?* You are the author of your life, not just its subject. The more interesting path compounds — and if it turns out wrong, you're at least fun at dinner parties. Use to break analysis ties, not to justify recklessness.

## Ask for help (the Sivers move)

When the user wants to outsource thinking to someone smarter. Make them earn it first: write the dilemma tightly — context, problem, options, your take on each. Then predict what the mentor would say and pre-answer it. Often the answer becomes obvious before the message is ever sent. The act of writing it clearly *is* the tool.

## A change of perspective is worth 50 IQ points

When the user is grinding the obvious framing and getting nowhere. The man parked his Rolls in the bank vault for two weeks by taking a $5,000 loan against it — cheapest parking in New York. Ask: *what is this situation actually an opportunity to do, if I stop reading it the default way?* Reframe the problem itself before grinding harder on it.

## The Swedish House Mafia technique

When the user is alone with a problem that needs more minds. Collect the smartest people they know, tell them the problem ("here's my 3rd-world jail cell"), shut out distractions, and rally ideas back and forth like tennis — taking immediate action on the best ones. A live problem deserves a live room.
